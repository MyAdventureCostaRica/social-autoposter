## The Trail Running Expedition (Episode 01, Trail Running)
Tagline: Run the spineof a country. — Nine days. Six athletes. Nine summits above three thousand metres, a self-supported queen stage descending into San Isidro de Dota, and a farewell dinner that no-one wants to end.
Region: Talamanca → Osa Peninsula. 9 days, 6 to 8 athletes, about 93 km.
Bookable departures: 5 – 13 December 2026; 20 – 28 February 2027; 7 – 15 August 2027.
Founder's line: "Every kilometre is one we have personally run." (Esteban Umaña, expedition leader)
Where: From the Cordillera de Talamanca at 3,000 m, descending the Pacific slope through cloud forest in the Dota Valley, out to the central Pacific coast, and south into the Osa Peninsula.
It begins above the tree line. The Cordillera de Talamanca is the country's continental spine — an ancient, uplifted ridge of black and green that separates the Pacific watershed from the Caribbean. The route opens on the spine itself, in the páramo above three thousand metres, then drops to the Pacific flank for the rest of the expedition — down through the Dota Valley, out to the central Pacific coast, and south into the Osa Peninsula. Nine summits on the first running day. A self-supported descent into a valley where families you have never met will hand you agua dulce and empanadas through their kitchen doors. Esteban has been refining this sequence for more than a decade.
Nothing about it is left to chance. Six to eight athletes, two professional guides, one dedicated photographer, and a logistical commitment as quiet as it is uncompromising. Each night you sleep in a lodge chosen for the way the morning light falls across the bedroom, not for its star count. Each day's distance is calibrated to the previous day's recovery. Each meal has a name and an origin and someone who made it.
You finish the way every long expedition should finish — at a long table, with people who started as strangers and end as the only ones who understand what just happened.
### The Trail Running Expedition · Dry Season: The Talamanca with the rain switched off: cold, clear mornings on the páramo, hard sun by nine, and views that reach both oceans from nine summits.
The dry season on the spine of Costa Rica: on the Nueve Cumbres you start in the cold — nights on the páramo sit at or below freezing, and the air above 3,400 metres is thin and clear enough to burn you by nine — and on a clear morning you run with both oceans in sight. In the first weeks of the season the mountain is still green from seven months of rain; by February the ridges are at their driest and clearest.
Down in Dota the quetzals are resident all year; from mid-February, when the wild avocados fruit, they come lower to feed, and a sighting beside the lodge at first light is a real possibility — never a promise. The Savegre runs low and clear through the Queen Stage descent. On the coast the dry season has cleared the sky: Manuel Antonio's trail runs bright and dry, and off Drake Bay the season belongs to the northern humpbacks, which breed on this coast from December to March.
Who it is for: runners who want the summits clear and the trails fast, and who accept that the dry season is Costa Rica's high season. Pack for two climates in one week — a warm layer for the páramo morning, and the lightest kit you own for the Osa.
### The Trail Running Expedition · Rainy Season: The Talamanca at full volume: early starts on the ridge, rain by mid-afternoon, and the Osa in whale season.
The rainy season on the Pacific slope keeps office hours: clear or dry mornings, cloud building towards midday, rain in the afternoon and early evening. So the expedition starts early. On the Nueve Cumbres we leave San José as early as the drive allows and aim to be off the ridge before the afternoon closes in; the Queen Stage descent into the Savegre runs through a cloud forest that has been wet for three months — moss swollen, every stream loud, the oaks in mist.
This far south there is no veranillo, the mid-year dry spell Guanacaste gets; August on the southern Pacific is simply the wet season, and the Osa is at its most alive. Around Drake Bay much of the rain falls at night; the days bring showers and rainbows, and from July to mid-November the bay is the breeding ground of the southern humpbacks — whales that feed off the Antarctic Peninsula and southern Chile — so a sighting with calves is a real possibility.
Who it is for: runners who do not mind weather, water on the trail and a country quieter than in the dry season. Expect mud, full rivers and the light that only comes after rain. Kit is a rain shell you trust and shoes that drain.

### Day by day

  After breakfast the support van climbs the Pan-American Highway into the Macizo del Cerro de la Muerte — a high section of the Cordillera de Talamanca and the continental divide of southern Costa Rica. The day's route is the local Nueve Cumbres traverse — 17.3 km along the ridgeline, summiting Jaboncillo, Estaquero, Sákira, Sábila, Zacatales, Asunción, Páramo and Cerro Buenavista (3,491 m, the highest point of this massif and the peak the highway is named for), with a final out-and-back to Cerro Frío. Nine in all — roughly 1,640 m of ascent, 1,190 m of descent. The trail starts at the treeline and crosses it several times along the ridge; every summit is in páramo, the tropical alpine grassland that in Costa Rica only exists above 3,000 m.
  From Buenavista the van picks you up and drops 1,500 m into the Río Savegre Valley — a different country at a different altitude. Tonight: Lauráceas Lodge. Ten chalets in the cloud forest, a family kitchen with trout and apples from the valley, and, with luck, the resplendent quetzal feeding outside your window in the morning.
- Day 3 · Cloud forest trails. (Cloud forest) · 9.2 km · +496 m / −495 m · cloud forest loop, 2,181–2,622 m · lodge: Lauráceas Lodge, San Gerardo de Dota · meals: Breakfast, dinner

Today's run is a 9.2 km loop from Lauráceas — climbing 500 m through montane oak forest on the eastern boundary of Los Quetzales National Park to roughly 2,625 m, then descending back to the lodge. The trail runs under moss-draped robles (Quercus) and aguacatillos — wild avocados in the Lauraceae family, which is what the resplendent quetzal feeds on, and why San Gerardo holds the largest year-round quetzal population in Costa Rica. The lodge takes its name from the same family of trees.
  The afternoon is yours. Rest at the lodge, walk down to the San Gerardo waterfall on the Río Savegre, take a birdwatching outing with a local naturalist — emerald toucanet, fiery-throated hummingbird, sometimes the quetzal at close range — or visit a family-run coffee finca in the valley.
- Day 4 · The Queen Stage. (Queen Stage) · 25 km · +1,380 m / −2,351 m · self-supported · lodge: Hotel Parador, Manuel Antonio · meals: Breakfast + local snacks · evening free
  The signature day. Twenty-five kilometres point-to-point from Armonía Ambiental in Providencia (~1,800 m). A steep climb out of the village opens the day; the first seven kilometres undulate along the high ridge to a top of 1,910 m, and then a long descent drops more than a thousand metres into the cloud forest. La Chaqueta is the first clearing in the mountain — you reach it as the trees give way. The trail follows the Río Savegre down through the valley below, then closes with a final 4 km, 455 m climb into the Zona Protectora Cerro Nara. The finish line is Ranchos Tinamú (~814 m), a community-run rural-tourism lodge in San Isidro de Dota named after the great tinamou whose call carries through this forest. Total: roughly 1,380 m of ascent, 2,350 m of descent — self-supported in the sense that no van follows you.
  What the day actually is: at three or four houses down the route, mountain families open their kitchens to runners — coffee, agua dulce, fruit, an empanada, a chair in the shade. The full story is in the Field Notes ('The Queen Stage, told slowly'). After Ranchos Tinamú the support team continues you out to Hotel Parador on the Manuel Antonio coast — five hectares of rainforest above the Pacific, where the next two nights are. Tonight dinner is yours: recover quietly at the hotel, or tell us the kind of table you want in town and we'll have it booked.
- Day 5 · Coast and recovery. (Pacific coast) · 9.5 km · +425 m / −429 m · coastal singletrack · lodge: Hotel Parador, Manuel Antonio · meals: Breakfast · evening free in town
  Trade mountains for coastline on the Reto MAE — Manuel Antonio Extreme — the local 9.5 km coastal trail and the route of an annual November race. From Quepos south to the gates of Manuel Antonio National Park, the path snakes through rainforest, climbs onto cliff-top bluffs, and drops onto seven Pacific beaches in sequence: Rocosa, Tulemar, La Vaca, La Macha, Biesanz, and the rest. Don't let the sea-level start fool you — the day accumulates around 425 m of gain over short, sharp pitches to each viewpoint.
  After the run, spend a relaxed afternoon at Hotel Parador — five hectares of rainforest, cliff-top pools above the Pacific. Optional: a guided visit to Manuel Antonio National Park (three monkey species including the endemic Central American squirrel monkey, three-toed sloths, agoutis), or sunset kayaking along the coast. Tonight, dinner is yours — Manuel Antonio is the one town on this route with a real choice of tables. Tell us the kind of evening you want and we'll have it booked.
- Day 6 · Into the wild. (Drake Bay) · 16.6 km · +285 m / −270 m · coastal jungle singletrack · lodge: Corcovado Wilderness Lodge, Drake Bay · meals: Breakfast, lunch, dinner
  After breakfast, journey south to the Sierpe River and board a boat that winds out through the Sierpe-Térraba mangroves — the largest mangrove system in Central America — into the open Pacific, and around the headland into Bahía Drake. From the village of Agujitas, the day unfolds along the Pacific coastal trail: 16.6 km of singletrack through the Punta Río Claro National Wildlife Refuge, tracing hidden coves, headlands, and stream crossings as the path slips deeper into the Osa Peninsula. Scarlet macaws in the beach almonds — the Osa holds the country's largest population.
  The run ends where you sleep: Corcovado Wilderness Lodge — 76.5 hectares of rainforest, two kilometres of oceanfront, bordered on three sides by 182,000 hectares of national park and reserves. Off-grid, accessible only by boat, all meals included. Tonight: monkey calls from the canopy, the sound of waves through the open windows, and a long table for dinner.

## The Gravel Expedition (Episode 02, Gravel Cycling)
Tagline: Ride the edgeof a peninsula. — Nine days. Six riders. Coast to coast across the Nicoya Peninsula over three bases — Santa Teresa, Nosara and Tamarindo — on the gravel that connects its wildest beaches.
Region: Nicoya Peninsula — Santa Teresa · Nosara · Tamarindo. 9 days, 6 to 8 athletes, about 356 km.
Bookable departures: 23 – 31 January 2027; 3 – 11 July 2027.
Founder's line: "The Nicoya Peninsula does not ride like Europe. It does not ride like anywhere." (Esteban Umaña, founder)
Where: Coast to coast across the Nicoya Peninsula — from the gulf-side ferry landing at Paquera, up the wild, undeveloped middle coast, into the mountains above Nosara, and on to the Guanacaste surf coast at Tamarindo. Ridden over three bases, hotel to hotel.
It begins with a crossing. The expedition gathers the night before in San José — welcome dinner, bikes fit and tuned. After breakfast you cross the Gulf of Nicoya to Paquera and ride: low and rolling past Tambor and Bahía Ballena, through Cóbano, and over a last set of hills to the Pacific surf at Santa Teresa, your first base.
From Santa Teresa, a climbing day — out of Lepanto and hard up a forested ridge in the green interior of the peninsula, then down its spine and back to the coast. Then the long one: a hundred kilometres up the wildest stretch of Nicoya, past Coyote, San Miguel, the turtle beach at Camaronal and the little art town of Punta Islita, to Nosara in the Blue Zone.
Nosara holds the queen stage — north to the Ostional refuge, then a hard turn inland and up to a high point around 950 m, all of the trip's climbing in one loop. The last riding day runs up the Guanacaste surf coast — San Juanillo, Playa Negra, Avellanas — to Tamarindo and the final base.
You finish on the Pacific, with people you started as strangers and end as the only ones who understand what just happened.
### The Gravel Expedition · Dry Season: The Nicoya Peninsula in its dry season: barely any rain, offshore wind in the mornings, straw-coloured hills, and every river crossing a dry bed or a shallow ford.
The dry season on the Nicoya Peninsula is heat, wind and open sky. Late January sits inside the driest stretch of the driest region in the country, and inside the season of the trade winds, which blow offshore in the mornings; the dry forest along the coast drops its leaves and the hills go the colour of straw. The offshore wind grooms the surf.
The wild coast between Santa Teresa and Nosara is a dry-season road: the river-mouth crossings are dry beds or shallow fords. Ostional's arribadas run almost every month, and in the dry season they are the small ones — the route rides past the refuge, so a sighting is luck and timing, never the programme.
Who it is for: riders who want firm, fast gravel and a dry-season week, and who will take heat and wind for it. Start early and drink more than you think.
### The Gravel Expedition · Rainy Season: The Nicoya Peninsula green: sunny mornings, an afternoon shower, evening light — and the wild coast taken inland around its river mouths.
The rainy season on the Nicoya Peninsula is a different place. The first rains of May turn the straw hills green; by July the coast is at full colour and the riding is cooler. The day has a shape: sun through the morning, cloud building towards midday, a shower in the afternoon, then light again. July is veranillo month in Guanacaste — the dry spell usually settles in from mid-July — so we ride on the rainy-season rhythm, not the lull.
The wild coast becomes a river story. In July the line leaves the beach at the big river mouths and goes round by the inland road, so the day's distance and climbing differ from the January line. At Ostional the big-arribada season is opening: from July to November hundreds of thousands of olive ridleys come ashore over three or four nights, the largest events in September and October — the route rides past the refuge, so a sighting is luck and timing. The south swell makes the surf bigger, and the sea runs less clear after rain.
Who it is for: riders who want the coast quieter than in the dry season and a shell in the bar bag. Expect green everything and an inland line around the river mouths.

Local people call the country it crosses the Camino Playa El Rey: it begins in the páramo of the Cerro de la Muerte — tropical alpine grassland that exists only above three thousand metres — and it ends on a beach with no paved road to it, where the Savegre lets go of the mountains. The Savegre is one of the cleanest rivers in Central America, and for three days it is also the trail: you cross it twice in a day — on a hanging bridge, then on a cable strung bank to bank — and once more the day after, on a hanging bridge a hundred and twenty-seven metres long at Los Campesinos.

### The Hiking Expedition · Dry Season: The mountain at its greenest with the rain switched off: cold, clear mornings on the páramo and a valley drying by the day.
The dry season begins in December, and its first days are the best of both seasons. Seven months of rain have left the Talamanca greener than at any other time of the year, and the rain itself is shutting down — a shower some afternoons, clear mornings, and on the páramo the first cold dawns of the season, at or below freezing above 3,300 metres, with sun that burns by nine in the thin air.
Down the valley the streams are still full but falling, the trail firming underfoot, the Savegre dropping by the week under the hanging bridge and the andarivel. Quetzals are resident in the Dota all year; December sits before the fruiting season, so seeing one is a gift rather than an appointment. On the central Pacific the clearer half of the year has just begun — the sky opens from late November — and December opens the northern humpbacks' breeding season on this coast.
Who it is for: walkers who want the cold-and-clear mountain and a coast in the light, and who accept that December is high season in Costa Rica. Layers for the páramo, a hat for the coast.
### The Hiking Expedition · Rainy Season: From the páramo to the sea in the wet: cloud forest at full saturation, the Savegre high under the crossings, and a coast with almost no one on it.
The rainy season made this valley. In June the Talamanca has been raining for six weeks and the cloud forest is wet and at its most alive: moss swollen on the oaks, streams loud under the trail, mist that comes and goes through the aguacatillos where the quetzals may still be feeding — their fruiting season runs from mid-February into June or July. The rhythm of the mountain is fixed: walk in the morning, watch the cloud build, and aim to be at the lodge when the afternoon rain comes.
Lower down, the Savegre is a different river: it runs high and fast under the hanging bridge and the andarivel, and the village trails of Quebrada Arroyo run through jungle that has not dried since May. On the coast, Playa El Rey is at its wildest, the sky over the Pacific building its afternoon weather while you stand in the tide line. June is the quiet month — before the July holiday traffic, after the dry-season crowds have gone home.
Who it is for: walkers who want the forest as it really is, and who will take rain in the afternoon for a morning of mist in the oaks. Waterproofs, a dry bag, and no expectations of a tan.

## The founder's journal (published posts, his own words — paraphrase, never copy)
### "Nine summits before the valley" (2026-09-19, Trail running)
Costa Rica is not supposed to be cold. That is the first thing this day takes from you, and the first thing it gives back. The Nueve Cumbres traverse runs 17 kilometres along the ridgeline of the Macizo del Cerro de la Muerte, the continental divide of southern Costa Rica, and it never once comes down below 3,000 metres.

I have known this high country for about 15 years, and it still took me 4 attempts to get this traverse right. It is now my favourite highland pass in Costa Rica — the one that contradicts the country most completely — and San Gerardo de Dota, waiting down the other side, is my favourite town in it. I did not bring anyone up here until I knew I had it.

## The drive up

On the expedition this is Day 2. Breakfast first, then the support van climbs the Pan-American Highway out of the valley and into the high country of the Cordillera de Talamanca. The road does the early work; the day's climbing — about 1,640 metres of it, against 1,190 metres of descent — belongs to the trail.

## Where the trees stop

The trail starts at the treeline and spends the whole day arguing with it — dipping back under the canopy, breaking out again into páramo, the tropical alpine grassland that exists in Costa Rica only above 3,000 metres. It is a landscape most visitors never learn this country has: low wind-shaped scrub, lichened rock, dwarf bamboo, air that bites at dawn and burns by mid-morning. The Pacific slope falls away on one side. On a clear December morning the clouds sit below you, not above.

## Nine names

The summits come in order like beads on a cord: Jaboncillo, Estaquero, Sákira, Sábila, Zacatales, Asunción, Páramo, Buenavista — and a final out-and-back to Cerro Frío makes nine. None of them requires your hands. All of them require your legs to still be honest, because a ridgeline never descends for long.

What I remember from this ridge is not a summit. It is the scouting: days up there under rain, at 2 °C. That is not a sentence this country is supposed to allow.

## Buenavista

Cerro Buenavista, at 3,491 metres, is the highest point of this massif — the peak the highway is named for. I should be precise here, because this cordillera invites exaggeration: the highest mountain in the Talamanca, and in Costa Rica, is Chirripó, further southeast. Buenavista is not that. What it is: the roof of this particular day, with the radio towers to one side and, on the right morning, both oceans somewhere in the haze.

## Down to the valley

From Buenavista the van is waiting, and the day performs its last trick: a drop of some 1,500 metres into the Río Savegre Valley, which is a different country at a different altitude — orchards, trout ponds, cloud forest closing back overhead. Tonight is Lauráceas Lodge: 10 chalets in the forest, a family kitchen, trout and apples that grew a few hundred metres from your plate.

The first thing I order is agua dulce — raw cane sugar dissolved in hot water, after 17 kilometres above 3,000 metres.

## Why this day

Because páramo is the rarest kind of ground this country owns, and the only way to spend a morning on it is on foot. Because nine summits sounds like bravado until you realise the whole ridge sits above 3,000 metres, and then it sounds like arithmetic. And because the expedition needs its athletes to meet the Talamanca on the second day — everything that follows, the queen stage included, descends from here.

### "The Queen Stage, told slowly" (2026-06-05, Trail running)
This is not the diary of a single morning. It is 25 kilometres I have been returning to for years — scouting it alone, guiding a group of runners down it for the first time in 2022, walking it on hiking trips, sleeping along it more than once. Told the way the route deserves: slowly, and only what I know to be true.

## Leaving in the dark

On a scouting trip I sleep at Armonía Ambiental itself — too rough for the comfort level we promise on an expedition, exactly right for the work. The alarm goes at 4:30, we are moving by 5:30, and the trail starts at the door. On a tour the day begins softer: the group sleeps in San Gerardo de Dota, takes breakfast between 6:00 and 7:00, and drives over to Providencia to meet the trailhead — 1,788 metres, the air still cold enough to argue with.

The route does not begin generously. People who study the profile assume a descent — we are, after all, leaving the high country for the valley — but the first 6 kilometres refuse to commit. The trail rolls along a forested ridge, full-on forest, climbing nearly as much as it gives back, until it touches the day's ceiling: 1,910 metres, around kilometre 6. There are no views handed out up here. The forest keeps them.

## The long descent

From the high point the trail finally tips, and the next hours are a controlled fall — more than 1,000 metres of it. By kilometre 8 you are at 1,616 metres and the cloud forest is loosening its grip; the air warms by degrees, the green changes register, and somewhere on the way down you take off the layer you swore you'd need all day.

## La Chaqueta

Around kilometre 15 the trees give way to the first house of the route. La Chaqueta is what the people here call this corner of the mountain — it belongs to Piedras Blancas, in San Isidro de Dota. I have never learned where the name comes from.

Xinia lives here with her husband. The trail passes directly in front of the house, crosses a few open fields, and slips back into the forest. I have run past this kitchen, hiked past it, spent nights nearby on overnight trips — and what comes over the doorsill is the reason this day cannot be replicated by any van-supported tour: picadillo, gallos, fresh fruit juice, agua dulce, pineapple empanadas. At 3 or 4 houses along this route, families do this — a little something from their homes, handed to people on foot.

## Down the valley

Past La Chaqueta the country opens into cattle fields. Not far on, back in the trees, a small footbridge takes you across the river for the first time. A few kilometres later the land opens again onto another house among open fields and patches of forest; a few kilometres more and there is a house down by the river with an artisanal sugar-cane mill — everything in it worked by hand.

The trail runs open along the river from there, until a bigger bridge crosses the Savegre into the centre of Piedras Blancas. It is a small community, and the path widens as if to acknowledge it.

## Don Hernán's crossing

A few kilometres past Piedras Blancas, right by the river, you reach Don Hernán's place — and the crossing this route is quietly famous for. There is no bridge here. You cross the Savegre in a basket strung on cables, pulling yourself over the water hand over hand.

The river here is the lowest point of the entire day: 359 metres, around kilometre 21. Kingfishers are always possible along this water — I cannot promise them, and I would not trust anyone who did.

## The last climb

What remains is honest and steep: the final 4 kilometres climb 456 metres on an open fire road toward the Zona Protectora Cerro Nara. After 21 kilometres your legs are not the legs that left Armonía — but the road looks back the whole way, over the mountains and the river valley below, and the views do a fair share of the carrying.

## Ranchos Tinamú

The day ends at 814 metres, at Ranchos Tinamú — a lodge that receives hikers off the Ruta Sukia and the Camino Playa El Rey. You clean up, you eat something, and on an expedition the van carries you onward to the Manuel Antonio coast. The Pacific never shows itself on this trail; it waits for that drive out, which feels right. One thing at a time.

## Why this is the queen stage

Real mountain trails. The good old countryside and its people. Self-sufficient from the first kilometre to the basket at Don Hernán's, with small Costa Rican treats as fuel for the road — and singletrack views that you earn rather than buy. That is the queen stage. Everything else on this expedition is built around being able to offer you this one day.

### "The long way back" (2026-06-21, Personal)
This is not a trail report. There is no route to map, no summit, no village kitchen handing food across the doorsill. It is the hardest thing I have written for this site, and I am writing it the way I write everything here: slowly, and only what I know to be true.

A few days ago I had a heart attack. I am still in the hospital as I begin to put this down. I want to keep it, the way I keep the routes — honestly, before the edges go soft.

## The pressure that didn't make sense

We came to San José on Saturday for the marathon. A little before 6 p.m., I felt a light pressure in my chest. Not pain, exactly. Just a sensation I didn't know what to do with.

We went out to eat and it stayed with me. I ate badly, uncomfortable in my own body, and on the walk back to the hotel the pressure began to climb into something else. By the time I reached the room I couldn't do the simple thing in front of me — pack my bag — without stopping.

## The room I couldn't get comfortable in

I tried to lie down. I tried it every way a person can try: on my back, on my side, curled, flat. There was no position that the pain would forgive. That is the detail I keep returning to. A pulled muscle lets you find an angle where it eases. This let me find nothing.

I called the person I love most and told her what I was feeling. She didn't hesitate: call 911. I called. There was no ambulance available, but the agent told me clearly — do not stay in that hotel room. Get to an emergency room if you can.

And here is the part I have to be honest about, because it is the part that could have killed me. For a moment I thought: maybe it passes if I just sleep. Maybe I'm making too much of this. I stood in that room and genuinely weighed lying down against walking out the door.

I walked out the door.

## Triage

At the emergency room I described it as best I could, and they ran an ECG. The doctor read it, left the room without a word, and came back with a gurney. He told me, with the kind of plainness you don't argue with, that I was having a heart attack.

I was awake for all of it. That is the strange thing I keep meeting in myself — I was lucid the entire time, watching it happen to me.

## The acute ward

They took me to the shock room to stabilize me, and after a long stretch of needles and every monitor in the universe attached to my body, they moved me to the acute ward — the room for sudden cases, the ones with a real chance of dying without warning. They held me there through the night and brought me back to stable.

I did the arithmetic later and it frightened me more than the night itself: the pain had started before 6 p.m., and I wasn't truly under care until close to 10 p.m. Something like 4 hours of my heart in trouble before treatment reached it.

## What the tests said

There was active damage to the heart muscle. An echocardiogram. An ejection fraction of 56% — the share of blood the heart pushes out with each beat, lower than it should be for someone built the way I am built. They referred me for a catheterization to place a stent and open the artery they assumed was blocked.

Then came the waiting. A cancelled procedure, a list I wasn't on, days stacking up in a public system where my case, stable now, had quietly slid down the order of urgency. I lay there doing the math on private hospitals and insurance and who pays for what — which, it turns out, was only ever me.

## Clean

When the catheter finally went in, they found nothing. No blockage. No obstruction. The procedure that was meant to fix the problem instead became the thing that told us there was no blockage to fix.

Sit with that for a second, because I had to. I am an endurance athlete. My resting heart rate is 38. And I had a genuine heart attack — confirmed, troponin and all — through arteries that were wide open.

The name for that is a heart attack with non-obstructed coronary arteries, and the cause they suspect is myocarditis: an inflammation of the heart muscle itself, most often set off by a virus the body was busy fighting somewhere I never noticed. The cardiologist was honest in a way I respected — the heart looks fine, and yet a heart attack happened. They referred me to an MRI and to cardiac rehabilitation.

## What I know to be true

I stayed lucid the whole way through because I needed to understand. I asked the nurses and the doctors to explain every number, every word — ejection fraction, troponin, the strange acronyms. I like to understand. Lying in that bed, understanding was the one thing I could still do for myself.

I know now that being sick is not a thing you choose. For days I kept apologizing — to work, to people waiting on me — as if I had decided to step away. I hadn't. My heart decided.

And I know that two small things stood between me and a much worse story: a person who said *call 911* without a second of doubt, and a voice on the phone who said *don't stay in that room*. I almost stayed in that room.

## The road back

So this is where the road back begins — from a hospital bed, with a heart that looks healthy and just proved it isn't yet, and an MRI and a rehab program ahead of me.

I am not going to pretend I know the whole route. I don't. But I know how to do this part, because it is the only thing I have ever really done: start at the trailhead, move at the pace the day allows, and not lie to myself about the terrain.

There will be a first kilometre back. Then a long one. And somewhere down that road, a start line again, and a group of people behind me on a mountain in the dark, waiting for first light. I intend to be there. Slowly, and only what is true — one step, and then the next.
